Use it for real

The lessons teach ideas one at a time. Real playing uses them together. Here are the three things you'll actually do with a guitar, step by step, with a link to the part of this page that trains each step.

1 · Work out a song by ear

  1. Find home. Listen to the end of the song or chorus. Hum the note it settles on, then find that note on the low E string.
  2. Major or minor? Strum the major and minor chord on that note. Whichever fits the ending is the key: G, or E minor, and so on.
  3. Get the slice. The key gives you six likely chords. In G: G, C, D, Am, Em, Bm.
  4. Try them in order of likelihood. For each change, try the 4 and 5 first, then the 6, then the 2 and 3. Bright chord means major, dark means minor.
  5. Write it down as numbers. "Verse: 1 5 6 4. Chorus: 4 1 5 5." Now you can play it in any key and you'll remember it.
  6. Find where to solo. Home note on the low E string, index finger there, minor pentatonic route. For a major key, use the relative minor.

Why this works: most songs stay inside one key's six chords, so knowing the key turns guessing among dozens of chords into choosing among six.

2 · Write a four-chord song

  1. Pick a key that suits your voice. Sing a comfortable low note and a comfortable high note; start with G or C and move it later.
  2. Pick four chords from the slice. Start and end on the 1. Classic shapes: 1 5 6 4, 1 4 5 4, 6 4 1 5 for a sadder feel.
  3. Give it a strum. Play the four chords for one bar each until the loop feels like a song, not an exercise.
  4. Find a melody from the chord notes. Hum over the loop. Land on a note of each chord on beat 1, like the solo plan.
  5. Make a chorus that lifts. Start the chorus on the 4, or end the verse on the 5 so it pulls into the chorus.
  6. Move it to fit your voice. Transpose every chord together, or use a capo to keep easy shapes.

Why this works: chords from one slice always fit together, so you can spend your effort on rhythm, melody and words.

3 · Jam with a friend or a backing track

  1. Agree the key and the numbers. "It's 1 5 6 4 in G" is all anyone needs to say.
  2. If they strum open chords, don't copy them. Play the same chords higher up with other shapes, so the two guitars sound bigger together.
  3. When it's your turn to solo, put your hand in place first. Home note on the low E string, first-position route.
  4. Aim for the chord notes. Land on the 3rd of each chord as it arrives, fill the gaps with scale notes, and leave some space.
  5. No friend handy? The loops in lessons 5 and 6 are backing tracks. Change the key and loop at the top, including the minor-key loops.
  6. Record it on your phone and listen back. Can you hear the chords change in your solo? That's the test.

Why this works: numbers let you talk, CAGED shapes let you fit around another player, and target notes make your solo belong to the song.

Where this is goingThe goal is to understand what you play: work out songs by ear, write progressions that sound right, find any chord anywhere on the neck, and solo so it sounds like it belongs to the song.

Why this orderLessons 1 and 2 give you the language: which chords belong together and why. Lessons 3 and 4 give you the map: where those chords are on the neck. Lessons 5 and 6 turn the map into melody: scales you can travel through and solos that follow the chords. Practice turns each lesson into a reflex, a few minutes a day. CAGED: one chord, five shapes

Every major or minor chord can be played in five places up the neck, and those places are the open C, A, G, E and D shapes moved up. They always come in that order and they link together, so the neck becomes five overlapping zones around one chord. Learn the chord tones in each zone and every scale position hangs off them. In practice only the E and A shapes are played as full barre chords; the C, G and D shapes are played as small chords on the top strings, or with a capo, and mostly used as a map of where the notes are. How to work with it

  • Play the chord in one shape, then its arpeggio, then the scale around it. Stay inside the shaded zone.
  • Walk the same chord up the neck through all five shapes without stopping. Then do it in a new key; only the starting shape changes.
  • Each shape shares its root notes with the next one. Those shared roots are the hinges that connect the zones.
  • Minor works the same way. Every 3rd drops one fret, and that is the whole difference.

Guide tones

A reference tool. For the guided version, take lesson 6, Playing over chords.

The 3rd and 7th of a chord (its guide tones) decide whether it sounds major, minor or dominant. In good progressions they move by a half step or stay put. Solid dots are the guide tones of the current chord; rings are where they go in the next chord. Solo by landing on these on beat one and your lines will spell out the changes.

Chord-tone soloing drill

  • Step 1. Loop the progression. Play only the 3rd of each chord, on beat one, in this zone.
  • Step 2. Play the 3rd, then the 7th, of every chord. You are now playing a guide-tone line.
  • Step 3. Fill the gaps with scale notes, but still land on a guide tone as each chord arrives.
  • Step 4. Approach each target from a half step below or above. This is where it starts to sound like jazz and blues.
  • Step 5. Move to the next zone and repeat. Then change key.

Intervals are shapes

An interval is the distance between two notes. On guitar each interval is a fixed shape you can carry anywhere. Pick an interval, then click any fret to move the root.

Shapes worth memorising

  • Octave: two strings up, two frets up. Crossing the B string, three frets up.
  • Perfect 5th: one string up, two frets up. This is the power chord.
  • Major 3rd: one string up, one fret back. Minor 3rd: one string up, two frets back.
  • Perfect 4th: the same fret, one string up (except G to B).
  • The B-string rule: G to B is tuned a major 3rd apart, every other pair a 4th. Any shape that crosses from G to B moves up one fret.

    How to read tab

    Tab is a picture of the neck turned on its side. The six lines are the six strings. The top line is the thin high e string, the one nearest the floor when you hold the guitar. The bottom line is the thick low E. People get this upside down at first, so check it every time.

    A number is the fret to press on that string. 0 means the open string. You read left to right, one column at a time. Numbers stacked in one column are played together, like a chord or a strum.

    Tab shows where, not how long. Here the spacing and the player fill that in: the vertical lines mark bars of four beats, the cursor moves in time, and small letters under a column tell you the finger (1 = index, 4 = little finger), sl for a slide, ap for an approach note, or ↓ ↑ for strum direction.

    That short example is: open A string, then fret 2 on the D string, then fret 2 on the G string, then all three together.

    How to use the builder

    1. Pick a note length: one beat, half a beat or a quarter of a beat. Every tap uses that length until you change it.
    2. Tap the neck. Each tap plays the note and adds it to the tab. Turn on guide dots to see a key's scale while you write.
    3. Chord mode stacks taps into one column, so you can write a chord, a double-stop or a strum. Press Finish chord to move on.
    4. Play it back slowly with Loop on, then speed it up. Copy as text gives you tab you can paste anywhere.

      The hold-down drill in plain words

      Held fingers stay down for the whole pass. The moving fingers play one at a time.

      • You don't hold all four. You hold only the fingers named after "Hold". "Hold 1, move 2 3 4" means finger 1 is held and nothing else.
      • Held (red dot with a ring): press it, pick it once so you know it rings, then leave it pressed until the pass ends. Don't lift it and don't pick it again.
      • Moving (white dots): these take turns, one finger at a time, in the order shown. Each one lands, plays its note, then lifts a few millimetres before the next one lands. Never two moving fingers down together.
      • Start with "Hold 1, move 2 3 4", then "Hold 4, move 1 2 3". Leave the ones where the ring finger moves alone until last; that's the hardest for everyone. The finger path above gives you the full order.

      Chromatic spider

      How the hold-down drill works

      Some fingers stay pressed on the fretboard while the others play. Your brain has to send "stay" to some fingers and "move" to their neighbours at the same time. The muscles that move the ring and little fingers are partly shared, so the held fingers want to lift and the moving ones want to drag their neighbours along. That fight is the feeling of your nervous system working out a new motor pattern, and it is exactly the thing this drill trains.

      Do it in this order

      1. Set the held fingers. Press each red dot with the finger number shown, close behind the fret wire, with just enough pressure to ring clearly. On the tab they are the red numbers at the start of each pass.
      2. Pick the held notes once so you know they ring. Then keep them pressed for the whole pass. They do not play again; they just stay down.
      3. Run the moving fingers on the string shown by the white dots, in the order of the finger numbers on the tab. Each pass moves up one string, and the held fingers never let go.
      4. Go slowly first. If a held finger lifts, a note buzzes or a neighbouring finger twitches, that is the drill doing its job. Drop the tempo until every held note rings all the way through, then use the tempo ladder.

      What to watch for

      • Lifting. The held fingers creeping off the string when the moving ones fire. Watch them, not the moving ones.
      • Tension. Squeezing with the thumb or locking the wrist. Relax between passes; shake the hand out.
      • Flying fingers. Keep the moving fingers close to the fretboard, not raised high.
      • Buzz or mute. A held finger leaning onto the string next to it and killing the note.

      Which combinations are hardest

      Roughly easiest to hardest: hold 1 and move 2 3 4, then hold 4 and move 1 2 3, then hold two together (1 2 or 3 4), and the alternating ones (hold 1 and 3, move 2 and 4) last. Hardest of all are the ones where a single finger moves alone, and the ring finger alone (hold 1 + 2 + 4, move 3) is the toughest, because it shares the most with its neighbours. When only one finger moves, it hops between two strings so it has to lift and replant on every note. Do the hard ones for a minute, not ten. Switch Held fingers sit on to work the same fingers on the other strings, and use Cycle through all combinations as a warm-up round.

      Accuracy checklist

      • One finger per fret. The fret number tells you the finger, so the fingering never changes mid-run.
      • Fingertips just behind the fret wire. Less pressure needed, less buzz, better tuning.
      • Keep fingers down and close. Lifted fingers should hover a few millimetres above the string, not fly off.
      • Strict alternate picking (down, up, down, up), even across strings. Use hammer-ons only in the finger-pair exercise if you want a legato day.
      • Slow is the shortcut. Start at a tempo where you make zero mistakes. Practising a mistake teaches the mistake.
      • Stop if anything aches. Shake out, relax the thumb, then continue slower.

      Three ideas that make everything faster

      • Scales hang off chords. Every scale position is a CAGED chord shape with passing notes around it. See the chord first and the scale stops being a box pattern.
      • Play the changes, not just the key. The same A minor pentatonic sounds generic over a whole tune. Landing on each chord's 3rd and 7th makes the progression audible in your solo.
      • Modes are colours over a chord. Don't learn them as seven new patterns. Learn which note sets each apart (Dorian 6, Mixolydian ♭7, Lydian ♯4) and aim for it.
